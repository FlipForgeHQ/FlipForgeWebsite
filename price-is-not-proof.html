Evidence Lab

Price is not proof.

FlipForge · October 20, 2026 · 5 minute read

A price tells you what someone asked, bid or reportedly paid. It does not prove what the card is worth, whether another buyer will pay that much, or whether buying it makes financial sense.

Three questions, not one

Most price tools blur three different questions together. Keeping them apart is the whole job.

QuestionWhat it really asks
What price was reported?A marketplace observation.
What does that price prove?Identity, whether the sale completed, how reliable the source is, and market conditions.
Is this a good buy at today’s price?Risk, liquidity, costs and your goals.

Why prices mislead collectors

More prices are not automatically better evidence. Ten mismatched, stale or unverified prices can be worth less than three solid sales of the exact card.

An asking price is not a sale.

Anyone can list a $200 card for $800. That sets the seller’s hope, not a buyer’s willingness to pay.

A sold listing may not show what the buyer paid.

When a seller accepts a Best Offer, eBay’s standard sold listings may show the original list price, and sellers report that the strikethrough marking the offer is not always applied. eBay’s Product Research tool shows the accepted offer price.

A reported sale may not have settled.

An auction can end with a winning bid that is never paid. On eBay, buyers have 4 calendar days to pay, and sellers can cancel an unpaid order after that, up to 30 days after the sale. A sale record alone is not proof that money changed hands.

Bidding can distort auctions.

eBay prohibits shill bidding, which it defines as bidding “to artificially increase its price, desirability, or search standing.” A surprisingly high result deserves a closer look, but a high price alone does not prove manipulation.

The same player is not the same card.

Year, set, card number, parallel, serial numbering, autograph, variation, grading company and grade all change the value. Comparing a PSA 10 Gold /10 with a PSA 9 base card gives a plausible-looking but meaningless price.

One expensive sale is not a market.

A card that sold once for $2,000 shows one buyer paid that, once. It does not show that several buyers exist at $2,000, or that the next seller will get it.

Yesterday’s price is not today’s market.

Injuries, performance, new releases, prospect hype, supply and sentiment all move prices. A genuine sale from months ago can be accurate history and still a poor guide to what you should pay today.

Even a legitimate sale can mislead

A low population is not proof of scarcity.

A population report counts the cards PSA has graded, not every copy in existence. Writing as PSA’s editor-in-chief, Joe Orlando put the question every collector should ask: “Is the card truly rare or have few people submitted the card for grading?”

A cert lookup does not rule out a fake label.

PSA states that verifying a certification number “does not eliminate risk,” and warns that criminals counterfeit PSA grading labels using real certification numbers. A cert check is one step, not a substitute for inspecting the card and the transaction.

Marketplaces report different kinds of price.

Heritage Auctions’ published prices realized include the buyer’s premium. At an illustrative 20% premium, a $1,200 result is a $1,000 hammer price, before any seller commission. Comparing it directly with a $1,200 eBay sale mixes two different kinds of price.

A selling price is not the seller’s profit.

eBay charges trading-card sellers without a Store 13.25% of the sale up to $7,500, then 2.35% on the portion above, plus a per-order fee of $0.40 (or $0.30 on orders of $10 or less). Shipping, promotion, taxes and grading can add more.

Example: why a $400 sale may be poor evidence

Illustrative card and hypothetical sales, not real market data.

Three sales of $400, $150 and $160 average $236.67. But two of the three sit near $155, and the median is $160. The $400 result could be a legitimate high-end sale, the wrong card, an unusual auction or an error. The numbers alone can’t say which. A good decision should explain:

  • Why one result is unusually high.
  • Whether all three sales are the exact same card and grade.
  • Whether the $400 sale actually completed and was paid for.
  • How much confidence three sales can justify.
  • What evidence is missing before buying.

The $400 sale shouldn’t be thrown out automatically either. An extreme result can be genuine. It should be checked, then counted, qualified or set aside, with the reason written down.

A $50 spread that loses money

Buying at $250 and reselling at $300 on eBay looks like a $50 gain. With eBay’s trading-card fees and $10 of other costs, it is a $0.15 loss.

Separate questions, separate answers

FlipForge checks these separately instead of folding them into one opaque score. A card can have a genuine $1,000 sale and still have thin demand and an unattractive $950 asking price.

The next, harder question

Genuine sale prices are important evidence. Price tools answer the first question: what did it sell for? FlipForge answers the next one: what does the evidence support, and what should you pay?
